Nura · The solution

One pole.
Its own sun.

Each Nura node makes, stores and spends its own energy. It checks on its neighbours every minute, and when one goes dark, AI tells a local fixer exactly what to bring.

The light node

Four parts. No grid.

We redesigned the streetlight around parts you can buy today, repair with hand tools and replace one at a time.

  1. Harvest

    A 30 W monocrystalline panel (21–30% cell efficiency) with a smart MPPT controller. It is waterproof and rated for sandstorms and 2,400 Pa wind, with a lifespan of up to 25 years.

  2. Store

    A 12 V 12 Ah LiFePO₄ battery, protected by three-stage charging against overcharge, over-voltage and short circuits. About 123 Wh usable: 1.5 nights on the full schedule, about 2.4 in low-battery mode.

  3. Light

    A 10 W, 1,200-lumen, IP65 outdoor LED lamp on a waterproof plug. A failed lamp is unplugged and swapped in about 10 minutes, with no electrician needed.

  4. Sense

    An ESP32 + LoRa node with an INA3221 current/voltage monitor on the panel, battery and lamp lines, plus a silicon photodiode that checks the lamp is really lit. It pings the light to its left and right, and reports to the zone gateway.

Sensor mapping

Every light watches its neighbours

Each light pings only the light to its left and to its right. That is all it takes to map a whole street.

  • Outage: if A can't reach B from the left and C can't reach B from the right, B is out.
  • Link fault: if only one side fails, the node is alive and the radio path is blocked. It is flagged, not dispatched.
  • Lamp fault: if the node still answers but its photodiode reads dark at night, the lamp has failed while the radio still works.
  • Segment outage: two or more neighbours out together usually means vandalism or conflict damage. The case is escalated.
AI that dispatches

From dark pole to repair email

A free, open-source Ollama model runs on a small computer at the zone gateway, fully offline. Fixed rules decide whether a light is out. The model explains why, and writes the local fixer a plain-language repair guide.

  1. Ping

    Every 60 s, each node pings its left and right neighbours and reports its battery, charge current and light level.

  2. Detect

    The gateway applies the outage rules. The rules make the decision; the AI is never trusted to decide whether a light is out.

  3. Diagnose

    Ollama reads the node's last telemetry. Zero panel current in daylight points to the panel, a battery that won't hold charge to the battery, a dark photodiode to the LED.

  4. Dispatch

    An email (or SMS) goes to the zone's local fixer: which pole, the likely cause, step-by-step fix, tools and parts to bring, and safety notes.

  5. Confirm

    When both neighbours can ping the repaired node again, the incident closes automatically.

The sensors

Measure the power, not the sky

A streetlight doesn't need to know how bright the sun is. It needs to know whether energy is flowing into the battery and whether the lamp is actually glowing. So every Nura light carries just two sensors: a current monitor on its power wires and a photodiode under the lamp.

The main sensor · about $4

INA3221 current and voltage monitor

What it measures
Real power on three wires: the solar panel coming in, the battery charging and draining, and the lamp going out.
Where it sits
Inside the sealed electronics box, wired between the charge controller, the battery and the lamp.
Why it suits the Sahel and East Africa
No moving parts and no window to the outside, so sand and rain can't reach it, and it's rated to 125 °C, well above the 60 °C a sealed box reaches in full sun. It also catches what a light sensor misses: a cracked or sand-covered panel reads zero current even on a bright day.
Setup
0.05 Ω shunts on the panel and battery wires, 0.1 Ω on the lamp wire. Read every 5 seconds.
The lamp check · under $1

BPW34 silicon photodiode

What it measures
Whether light is really coming out of the lamp.
Where it sits
Under a short hood on the lamp mount, aimed at the lamp's own pool of light.
Why it suits the Sahel and East Africa
Silicon stays accurate in heat and doesn't wear out in strong sun. Every 10 minutes at night the lamp blinks off for a fifth of a second, too fast for anyone to notice, so moonlight and passing headlights cancel out and only this lamp is measured.
Setup
A 1 MΩ resistor into the sensor board's analog input.

Nothing else is needed. The solar panel doubles as the dawn and dusk switch: above about 8 V it's day, below about 2 V it's night. A tilt switch and a lid switch report tampering. The reasons for not using a basic light sensor or a sun tracker are in docs/Nebo.md §3.4.

Created for the people

Owned by the city. Not by a corporation.

The network, the data and the dashboard belong to the municipality or neighbourhood committee. There are no licences, no cloud bills and no contractor lock-in.

  • Open hardware: the parts list and case design are public, so any workshop can build a node.
  • Offline AI: Ollama runs on the zone gateway, so there is no subscription and no data leaves the city.
  • Local fixers: repair guides are written for a trained resident with hand tools, not a specialist engineer.
  • Priority zones: refugee camps, school routes and clinic roads get lit and repaired first.
The printed case

Built to grip any pole

A single 3D-printed enclosure combines the solar tray, sealed electronics bay and plug-in LED lamp mount. A three-prong clamp locks it onto existing metal poles, including ones left standing after the grid failed.

  • Parametric: pole diameter, panel size and tilt are variables in the model.
  • UV-stable: printed in ASA or PETG, with gasket grooves to keep out dust and rain.
  • Tamper-aware: the clamp uses security screws, and the sensor node reports if the case is opened.
